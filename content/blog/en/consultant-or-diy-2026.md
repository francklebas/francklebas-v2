## Fair question, actually

In 2026, you can build a website in an evening. You describe what you want to an AI, and it generates it. Wix, Shopify, Lovable: it's all there, often for a few euros a month. So why pay anyone?

I'm Franck, a frontend developer since 2008. And I'll let you in on something: most of the time, that question is legitimate. I'm not going to tell you that you need me. I'm going to help you figure out whether you need anyone at all. Big difference.

## When doing it yourself is smart

Let's be honest. In these cases, keep your money:

- **You're testing an idea.** Not sure yet whether your business will take off? A homemade site is more than enough to find out.
- **You need a simple storefront.** Opening hours, an address, three photos, a phone number. You don't need a developer for that.
- **Your budget is close to zero.** An imperfect site that's online beats a perfect one that doesn't exist.
- **You enjoy tinkering.** If it's fun and you have the time, go for it. You'll learn a lot.

In all these cases, a consultant would be a luxury. And a good consultant will tell you so themselves.

## When free starts getting expensive

The problem isn't building the site. It's what happens afterwards. Here are the signs you've crossed the line:

- **Your time is worth more than the subscription.** Three weekends fixing a menu that breaks on mobile is three weekends without customers.
- **Nobody can find you.** The site exists, but Google ignores it. Building a site and making it visible are two different jobs.
- **It's slow.** Every second of loading time drives visitors away, especially on phones.
- **You want it to grow, and it can't.** Online booking, payments, a customer area: the platform can't keep up, or it charges you for every option.
- **You're locked in.** Your content and your customer data live with a vendor. The day they change their prices, you pay. Or you start over.

> **A short story.** At a SaaS company where I was lead frontend developer, we picked Storyblok as our headless CMS. Entry price: €29.99. Two years later, the bill was unilaterally raised to over €99.99. And automatic backups, included until then, were now only available on the higher tier. Spot the problem. We paid the price of lock-in, as we so often do without seeing it coming.

Sound familiar? The real cost of a website isn't building it. It's what it quietly makes you lose.

## What a consultant really sells in 2026

We used to pay developers to type code. Today, AI types the code. So what's left?

Judgment. Knowing what to build, and above all what not to build. I hate coding features that "might" be useful someday. That's time and money thrown out the window. Yours.

Picture a surgeon telling you: "The scalpel is easy, anyone can buy one." True. But you're not paying them for the scalpel. You're paying them to know where to cut.

Same thing here. A good consultant doesn't sell you hours. They save you from the costly mistakes, and they tell you when you don't need them.

## So yes, I use these tools too

A small confession: my own site, Ouverture, I built with Lovable. Yes, a developer with 18 years of experience using a "no-code" tool. And I'm not hiding it.

These tools are excellent for moving fast. They saved me the time it would have taken to build the structural work. And "structural work" is the right word: code is the heavy lifting, and that's where AI is faster than us humans.

But I also know exactly where they fall short:

- **They don't know your customers.** An AI imitates empathy perfectly. It doesn't replace someone who has actually watched your customers walk through the door.
- **They're accountable for nothing.** If it breaks on a Saturday night, nobody picks up the phone.
- **They're not as perfect as they're sold to be.**

The difference isn't the tool. It's knowing when it's enough, and when to take back control.

## In short

Do it yourself as long as it works. I seriously mean that. The day your site costs you more than it brings in, in time, customers or nerves, that's when an outside eye starts to pay for itself.

Tools change every six months, if not faster. Knowing what to do with them is what lasts.
